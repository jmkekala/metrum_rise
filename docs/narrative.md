# Narrative

The setting, the arc from backwoods to space launch, the political layer, and
the events that carry them.

This documents is about what the country of Metrum is, what the player is doing,
what the early game and the late game entail, and where the humour sits.

The comedic register is a serious simulation with political comedy satirizing the
curent state of the most-modern, neo-liberal world on top, in the style of Tropico.
NBut unlike Tropico, nothing here should ask the simulation to soften so that a
joke can land, if it does, it is wrong.

## The country

The country is Metrum. The player governs all of it.

The name of the game is the arc: Metrum rises, from backwoods to powerhouse.
The first town is a few hundred people in a region with one road out. The last
is a country launching rockets, building bleeding edge microprocessors, a country
that is a titan of film, the king of tourism, or .

That arc is why the opening is deliberately small, along with easing the player
into running multiple cities in multiple regions, and why the endgame is a space
program. Nothing about the early game should feel like a tutorial for the late
game; it should feel like the place the late game came from.

Twenty to thirty regions make a country. A small one, but a country, and the
player governs the whole of it. Calling this a city builder undersells what it is:
it is a country builder that happens to start with one town.

### The people

Metrum's people are an even mix of Black, white, Latin, Middle Eastern, and
Asian. Not a majority with minorities: an actual even mix, in every region and
at every income.

This is never explained, nor does it need to be. This is the best possible
neutral position. No founding myth accounts for it, no dialogue remarks on it,
no loading screen justifies it. It is simply what the country looks like, the
way a real country's composition is simply what it is to the people living there.

The setting supports this without arguing for it. An imaginary Mediterranean-esqe
region, as it allows an interesting and large inland sea with islands that will have
tropics, to snow packed and barren mountain ranges and dry inland deserts. The
mediterranean has been a nexus for as long as anyone remembers, and a country on
it would be mixed for the ordinary reason that people have always arrived.

### Media

Because every agent is real and the director watches them, the game can gather
genuine news from across the country: procedural events that actually happened
to actual people. This enables the creation of media companies that will actually
observe and report on your cites, instead of providing canned feedback like Cites:
Skylines' Chirper.

Media from outside your country will exist as well, and international events will
occasionally make the news. The news feed acts as the players informal gague for
how their country is doing, and another vessel to channel the games humerous
undertones.

## The arc

Rough hours for the average player:

| Hours | What the player is doing |
|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | One town, one region, one to two roads out |
| 10 to 40 | A second city, then a third; attention splits |
| 40 to 150 | Regions: unlock neighbors cheaply, or save for a distant one |
| 150 to 200 | The endgame, on two tracks |
| Post-game | Build as long as you want |

A power gamer might complete a win condition in 70 to 90 hours. Beyond the estimated playtime
for one country, it is a statement that the content has to hold if a player chooses to engage
for that lost or longer. This is a genre where single saves can absorb well over a thousand
hours for many poeple with no ending in sight at all.

The endgame is a set of victory conditions, different in kind:

- Industry: bleeding-edge microchip manufacture/Silicon Valley. The deepest resource
  chain in the game, needing materials no single cluster of regions holds, plus the power,
  water, transport, and skilled labor that fabrication demands. It is the industrial
  endgame because it is the hardest thing to supply.
- Science: a space launch. The other track, and the one that reads as an ending.
- Tourism: build Las Vegas/Paris. Build the worlds most desired vaction cites.
  Nightlife, gambling, beaches, national parks, etc...
- Entertainment: Hollywood. Create the worlds largest entertainment industry.
- Ideally 2-6 more win conditions should ship. Like civ, they are are collectively
  achievable and never end the game, its basically just how we throw the credits in from a
  purely mechanical standpoint.

All are gated by what the map actually holds rather than by an unlock table. The world the
player was given decides how far they have to reach. All conditions are possible, but the map
and the players early choices will largely dictate which ones are harder than others.

The endgame condition ends the objective, not the save.

The failure this targets is bigger than boredom, and every city builder in the
genre has it.

A player gets tired of a city and moves on and the save goes cold. They mean to
come back and almost never do, because there is nothing to come back to: the
city is static, filed away, exactly as they left it. Most players of these games
have a folder of abandoned saves behind them, I know I do, each one a place they
liked and cannot meaningfully return to.

Regional progression makes a city you left still exist. It keeps running, it
keeps growing under the conditions you left it, its mayor keeps making decisions,
and it is still yours. Going back is returning somewhere left running, not reopening
a photograph. Give players a single map, the size of a small to medium sized European
country, with every biome they could ever want, and they no longer have to abondon a
save because they want to build a different city ever again, they build it in accessory.

The rhythm that produces, and the reason each step arrives *before* the previous
one runs out:

- Around hour 10, before the first city goes stale, start a second in the same
  region. Attention splits.
- The new city takes the novelty; the first keeps running.
- Once the second is established an hour or two in and the thrill of founding it is
  spent, going back to the first is a return rather than a chore, because stuff
  happened and it now needs you.
- When both quiet down and the second matures, the player unlocks a third.
- When the region gets a little old, a new region opens: go make three to five more.
- When regions themselves go quiet, its time for the min-maxxers, the endgame options
  become possible now that you have a dozen cites across 4 regions.

Nothing is ever abandoned, because everything is still in the same country and
still running.

## The political layer

All cities have mayors who make decisions. A mayor is what makes a city keep
existing rather than paused when attention moves elsewhere, and what makes returning
to it feel like a return. When the player is currently on a city, that cities mayor
doesn't actually do much, but while away they make simple decisions that keep the
city afloat if left in good condition by the player, and flounders if the city was
already failing. The mayor may not create massive new developments, destoroy anything,
or majorly affect policy. They can oversee industry and expand zones if resources
deplete, manage public transpo, create small inconcequential neighborhoods and zoning
expansions, and affect other changes that are very specifically tuned to not make the
player feel like they were robbed of too much agency.

The player is not elected and cannot be voted out. The pressure is not electoral,
which means unrest, migration, and money have to carry it instead. A population
that hates the government expresses that by rioting, by leaving, or by refusing
to arrive, and all three are visible in the simulation rather than in a poll.

### The big companies

In other countries around the world there are companies which will be you end game
"rivals." The working titles are just that. They are all parodies of real world
companies that have gained reputations worthy of mocking.

**Testla** is a conglomerate spanning rockets, electric cars, and a social network, run by a
figure recognizable to anyone who has watched that industry, or who  has seen
the 5th Element. Played closer to the tech chief executive in *Don't Look Up*
than to any real villain.

They are the competitor for the science and tech endgame industries. They are
building toward the same things the player is, they are better funded, and they
announce it constantly. Whether the player's rocket or theirs launches first is
the shape of an endgame race. Losing the race is not a loss condition, only
a very smug press cycle.

**Disn't** is an entertainment and media giant that owns parks, studios, and a great
deal of the market. The satire plays on the founder's head and the freezing, on
owning an entire district in which the government has no real jurisdiction, and
on the more remarkable things such a company has actually arranged for itself.

The line is firm: no mouse, no allusion to their logo, their font, their
characters, or their properties. The resemblance is a media giant with parks
and market control, which is a category rather than a company, and everything
funny about it comes from corporate behavior rather than from intellectual
property. Technically we should be able to get away with far greater than this, and
perhaps this line changes later, but Disney is feroucious.

Beyond the two named giants, foreign companies approach throughout the game, on
and off, starting early. They want to move industry into the country, buy a
domestic company, or take a position in something the player has built.

Their offers range from predatory to genuinely decent, and the predatory ones
are the attractive ones. A young country short of capital is exactly the
counterparty a bad deal is designed for. The player who takes every early offer
gets a fast start and a country whose industry belongs to somebody else.

The design rule that keeps this honest: a bad deal must look good at the time
it is offered. An offer flagged as predatory is not a decision, it is a
warning label. The flavour of the interaction should give it away in a satirical
manner, but not too much.

### Border policy

Border policy runs the full range, from sealing the border entirely to fully
open. It is the North Korea to Emma Lazarus scale for now.

The mechanics of checkpoints, patrol, and the four border states live in the
network and border documentation. What belongs here is that border policy is a
political statement the population responds to, not only a migration
coefficient.

Openness is the ceiling and the immigration grants in `services.md` move the
rate underneath it. Paying skilled foreigners to arrive is a policy the
population has an opinion about, especially while domestic sims are out of work,
and it is the sort of thing the media layer picks up.

## Events that carry the story

### The bail-out

Early on, a neighboring region with a specialized industry runs into trouble and
asks for money. Bailing them out is expensive at a point when the player has
not much to spare. Letting them fail saves money now.

The industry they specialized in is one the player cannot build yet and will
need later. If the player let them fail, the late game is harder in the late game
as they will have to develop that industry on their own, and the reason is
legible: a decision made 100 hours earlier, for a good reason at the time, with
consequences invisible to the imperceptive. The game will offer some help, but
not hold the players hand.

It needs one guard: the loss has to be recoverable at greater cost, through
another region or an expensive substitute chain for a good deal, and a bad deal
must come with a nice up-front boon and escalating consequences later. Harder,
not fatal, or the mechanic becomes a trap that invalidates a save.

Events like this, and other similar kind sof events should happen every few hours
of gameplay.

### Archaeological sites

Expanding you city sometimes turns things up. During excavation or construction
the player will sometimes uncover an archaeological site, and then has to decide
what to do about it:
- Halt and excavate properly. The site is studied, the ground is tied up for
  a long time, and the project waits. What comes out of it can become a museum
  piece, a tourist draw, or a national monument.
- Work around it. Preserve the site in place and route the road, the
  foundation, or the pipeline around it. Cheaper than a full excavation and
  permanently awkward.
- Pave over it. Fastest and cheapest today. The find is destroyed, and the
  game does not stop the player, but the world remembers.

None of the three is the correct answer, and the game must not signal one.
Preservation costs time and money a country might not have and destruction is
free now but the sort of thing a population remembers.

What makes this worth having rather than a pop-up tax is that the outcomes
compound with systems that already exist. A properly excavated site feeds
tourism and the museum. A preserved one is a permanent constraint on the parcel
and the road network around it. A destroyed one is a political fact that the
media layer will report and the population will respond to. Frequency is low and
the trigger is the ground, not a timer. Sites are seeded into the world at generation,
so a given tile either holds something or does not, and the player finds out by
digging there.

A site can also be found on purpose. Survey teams looking for mineral deposits use
the same seismic tests and ground-penetrating radar, so a tile can be searched for
what is under it before anything is built on it. `terrain.md` owns the survey.

That gives the choice a second shape. Uncovering a site mid-construction is the
accident, with a project already underway and money already spent. Finding one by
surveying first is a decision made before committing, and a player who suspects a
region is worth searching can pay to know rather than pay to find out. The three
options are the same either way; what changes is how much is already sunk when
the player faces them.

The excavation and construction hooks that raise it belong to `earthworks.md`
and the building lifecycle; what belongs here is that the choice is political
and the consequences are long.

### Riots start with an incident

A sufficiently disgruntled population riots, and the trigger is an incident
rather than any pure meter or threshold release.

A city can be deeply unhappy for a long time without anything igniting. Then a
specific police incident occurs, a real interaction between real agents, and that
is what starts the riot. Not a meter crossing a line, not a timer expiring. A thing
that happened after the pressure was already there.

That gives the player something a meter never does: the ability to see conditions
building, and the knowledge that the spark is not scheduled. Two cities equally
unhappy can go a hundred hours apart, because one had the incident and the other
did not.

## Tone

- Serious simulation, political comedy on top.
- The humour is Tropico's political texture, not its islands, its elections, or
  its dictator fantasy.
- Parody targets institutions and corporate behavior, never a named real person.
- Consequence teaches, not tutorial prompts. When a player has ignored something
  important, the game makes it apparent by letting it matter, first in a way that
  shows the consequences without making the player feel them, the second gets no
  handicap.
