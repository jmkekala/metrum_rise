# Narrative

The setting, the arc from backwoods to space launch, the political layer, and the events that
deliver them.

This document is about what the country of Metrum is, what the player is doing, what the early game
and the late game entail, and where the humour sits.

The comedic register is a serious simulation with political comedy satirizing the current state of
the most-modern, neo-liberal world on top, in the style of Tropico. Unlike Tropico, the simulation
here never softens so that a joke can land. Where it does, that is wrong.

## The country

The country is Metrum. The player governs all of it.

The name of the game is the arc: Metrum rises, from backwoods to powerhouse. The first town is a few
hundred people in a region with one road out. The last is a country launching rockets, building
bleeding edge microprocessors, a titan of film, or the king of tourism.

That arc is why the opening is deliberately small and why the endgame is a space program. The
opening also eases the player into running multiple cities in multiple regions. The early game
should feel like the place the late game came from.

Twenty to thirty regions make a country. This is a country builder that starts with one town.

That country is `100,000 km2` of land along a Mediterranean-style sideways U wrapped around an
inland sea with dozens of islands. [`region.md`](region.md) owns the tier and [`terrain.md`](terrain.md) owns the
generator.

### The people

Metrum's people are an even mix of Black, white, Latin, Middle Eastern, and Asian. Every group holds
an equal share, in every region and at every income.

This is never explained. It is the best available neutral position. No founding myth accounts for
it, no dialogue remarks on it, and no loading screen justifies it. It is what the country looks
like.

The setting supports this without arguing for it. An imaginary Mediterranean-style region allows a
large inland sea with islands, tropics, snow-packed and barren mountain ranges, and dry inland
deserts. The Mediterranean has been a nexus for as long as anyone remembers, and a country on it
would be mixed for the ordinary reason that people have always arrived.

### Media

Every agent is real and the director watches them, so the game gathers genuine news from across the
country: procedural events that happened to actual people. Media companies observe and report on
the player's cities. Cities: Skylines' Chirper delivers canned feedback.

Media from outside the country exists as well, and international events occasionally make the
news. The news feed is the player's informal gauge for how their country is doing, and another
channel for the game's humour.

## The arc

Rough hours for the average player:

| Hours | What the player is doing |
|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | One town, one region, one to two roads out |
| 10 to 60 | A second city, then a third; attention splits |
| 60 to 120 | Regions: unlock neighbors cheaply, or save for a distant one |
| 120 to 200 | The endgame, 1 of X tracks |
| Post-game | Build as long as you want and complete other end-gae goals |

A power-gamer might complete a win condition in 100 hours. The content has to hold well past that:
this is a genre where single saves absorb well over a thousand hours with no ending in sight.

The endgame is a set of victory conditions, different in kind:

- Industry: bleeding-edge microchip manufacture, Silicon Valley. The deepest resource chain in the
  game, needing materials no single cluster of regions holds, plus the power, water, transport, and
  skilled labor that fabrication demands.
- Science: a space launch. The other track, and the one that reads as an ending.
- Tourism: build Las Vegas or Paris. Build the world's most desired vacation cities. Nightlife,
  gambling, beaches, and national parks.
- Entertainment: Hollywood. Create the world's largest entertainment industry.
- Ideally 2 to 6 more win conditions ship. Like Civilization, they are collectively achievable and
  never end the game; mechanically they are how the credits roll.

The map gates all of them, and no unlock table does. Every condition is possible on every map, and
the terrain plus the player's early choices decide which ones are harder.

An endgame condition ends the objective. The save continues.

Every city builder in the genre has this failure.

A player gets tired of a city and moves on and the save goes cold. They mean to
come back and almost never do, because there is nothing to come back to: the
city is static, filed away, exactly as they left it. Most players of these games
have a folder of abandoned saves behind them, I know I do, each one a place they
liked and cannot meaningfully return to.

Regional progression keeps a city you left in existence. It keeps running, it keeps growing under
the conditions you left it, its mayor keeps making decisions, and it is still yours. Going back
means returning somewhere still running. Give players a single map the size of a small to medium
European country, with every biome they could want, and they never have to abandon a save to build
a different city; they build it alongside.

The rhythm that produces, and the reason each step arrives *before* the previous one runs out:

- Around hour 10, before the first city goes stale, start a second in the same region. Attention
  splits.
- The new city takes the novelty; the first keeps running.
- Once the second is established an hour or two in and the thrill of founding it is spent, going
  back to the first is a return, because events happened there and it now needs the player.
- When both quiet down and the second matures, the player unlocks a third.
- When the region gets a little old, a new region opens: go make three to five more.
- When regions themselves go quiet, the endgame options open for the min-maxxers, with a dozen
  cities across four regions in hand.
  
#### Post-game Comtent

Currently, the post game content is limited to persuing the additional end-game goals. Each one
should provide an additional 40-80 hours for the player with some making others easier, totaling 
around 500-600 hours for the average goal oriented completionist, 300 for power-gamars, and well
over 1000 hours to casuals per save. For power-gamers, an achievement for completing multiple or
all end-game loops before other nations do a grind target.

Beyond completing all end-game loops, the post-game content should include loops unique to many
different specializations or industries, perhaps unlocked by completing the end-game content.
Posssible examples include:
- Surges of global transportation that tax the players infrastructure, and reward successful
  mitigation and upgrades with more income.
- Global disasters in neighboring countries causing waves of immagrants that test the players
  infrastructure.
- The volcano in the center of the player map goes off, destroying crops, interrupting flights,
  and damaging cities, putting all of the players disaster infrastructure to the ultimate test.

## The political layer

All cities have mayors who make decisions, keeping a city running while attention is elsewhere.
While the player is on a city, that city's mayor does little. While away, they make simple
decisions that keep the city afloat if the player left it in good condition, and flounder if the
city was already failing. The mayor may not create massive new developments, demolish anything, or
set policy. They can oversee industry and expand zones as resources deplete, manage public
transport, and create small neighborhoods and zoning expansions, tuned so the player keeps their
agency.

The player is not elected and cannot be voted out. Unrest, migration, and money carry the pressure
in place of an election. A population that hates the government expresses that by rioting, by
leaving, or by refusing to arrive, and all three are visible in the simulation.

### The big companies

In other countries around the world there are companies which become the player's endgame rivals,
all parodies of real world companies with reputations worthy of mocking. The names below are
working titles.

**Testla** is a conglomerate spanning rockets, electric cars, and a social network, run by a figure
recognizable to anyone who has watched that industry, or who has seen the Fifth Element. Played
closer to the tech chief executive in *Don't Look Up (2021)* than to any real villain.

They are the competitor for the science and tech endgame industries, building toward the same
things the player is, better funded, and announcing it constantly. Whether the player's rocket or
theirs launches first is the endgame race. Losing it costs the player a smug press cycle and
nothing else.

**Disn't** is an entertainment and media giant that owns parks, studios, and a great deal of the
market. The satire plays on the founder's head and the freezing, on owning an entire district in
which the government has no real jurisdiction, and on the more remarkable things such a company has
actually arranged for itself.

The line is firm: no mouse, no allusion to their logo, their font, their characters, or their
properties. The resemblance is to a category, a media giant with parks and market control, and the
humour comes from corporate behavior. Technically we should be able to get away with far greater
than this, and perhaps this line changes later, but Disney is ferocious.

Beyond the two named giants, foreign companies approach throughout the game, on
and off, starting early. They want to move industry into the country, buy a
domestic company, or take a position in something the player has built.

Their offers range from predatory to genuinely decent, and the predatory ones
are the attractive ones. A young country short of capital is exactly the
counterparty a bad deal is designed for. The player who takes every early offer
gets a fast start and a country whose industry belongs to somebody else.

A bad deal must look good at the time it is offered. An offer flagged by UI as predatory is a
warning label. The flavour of the interaction gives it away satirically, without spelling it out.

### Border policy

Border policy runs the full range, from sealing the border entirely to fully open. It is the North
Korea to Emma Lazarus scale for now.

The mechanics of checkpoints, patrol, and the four border states live in the network and border
documentation. Border policy is also a political statement the population responds to, on top of
its effect on the migration rate.

Openness is the ceiling and the immigration grants in `services.md` move the
rate underneath it. Paying skilled foreigners to arrive is a policy the
population has an opinion about, especially while domestic sims are out of work,
and it is the sort of thing the media layer picks up.

## Events

### The bail-out

Early on, a neighboring region with a specialized industry runs into trouble and asks for money.
Bailing them out is expensive at a point when the player has little to spare. Letting them fail
saves money now.

The industry they specialized in is one the player cannot build yet and will need later. If the
player let them fail, the late game is harder, because they have to develop that industry
themselves. A decision made 100 hours earlier, for a good reason at the time, stays legible as the
cause. The game offers some help and stops there.

It needs one guard: the loss has to be recoverable at greater cost, through another region or an
expensive substitute chain for a good deal, and a bad deal must come with an up-front boon and
escalating consequences later. The outcome stays survivable, so the mechanic never invalidates a
save.

Events of this kind should happen every few hours of gameplay.

### Archaeological sites

Expanding a city sometimes turns things up. During excavation or construction the player uncovers an
archaeological site, and then decides what to do about it:
- Halt and excavate properly. The site is studied, the ground is tied up for a long time, and the
  project waits. What comes out of it can become a museum piece, a tourist draw, or a national
  monument.
- Work around it. Preserve the site in place and route the road, the foundation, or the pipeline
  around it. Cheaper than a full excavation and permanently awkward.
- Pave over it. Fastest and cheapest today. The find is destroyed, the game allows it, and the world
  remembers.

All three are valid answers, and the game signals none of them.

The outcomes compound with systems that already exist. A properly excavated site feeds tourism
and the museum. A preserved one is a permanent constraint on the parcel and the road network
around it. A destroyed one is a political fact the media layer reports and the population responds
to. Frequency is low, and the ground triggers it. Sites are seeded into the world at generation,
so a given tile either holds something or does not, and the player finds out by digging there.

A site can also be found on purpose. Survey teams looking for mineral deposits use the same seismic
tests and ground-penetrating radar, so a tile can be searched before anything is built on it.
`terrain.md` owns the survey.

Uncovering a site mid-construction is the accident, with a project already underway and money
already spent. Finding one by surveying first is a decision made before committing, and a player
who suspects a region is worth searching can pay for the answer in advance. The three options stay
the same either way; the amount already sunk is what changes.

The excavation and construction hooks that raise it belong to `earthworks.md` and the building
lifecycle. The choice is political and the consequences are long.

### Riots

A city can be deeply unhappy for a long time without anything igniting. Then a police incident
occurs, a real interaction between real agents, and that starts the riot. No meter crosses a line
and no timer expires.

The player can see conditions building and knows the spark is unscheduled. Two cities equally
unhappy can go a hundred hours apart, because one had the incident and the other did not.

## Sequel sketch

A draft-board note. Nothing here is settled.

A science-fiction sequel hangs off the Mars launch. A player finishes MR1 at the space program
and carries that save into MR2, where the endgame content becomes the starting position. The
Mars side is a space-station minibuilder at a different scale from the country builder. One
possible coupling: the Mars settlement depends on launches from the home country continuing for
some period, so the first game keeps running underneath the second.

Open: whether the transition is a save import, a shared save, or a fresh start seeded from the
first game's endgame state. Whether the home country stays playable, stays simulated, or becomes
a launch schedule. What the minibuilder shares with the main engine.

## Tone

- Serious simulation, political comedy on top.
- The humour takes Tropico's political texture. Its islands, its elections, and its dictator
  fantasy stay out.
- Parody targets institutions and corporate behavior, never a named real person.
- Consequence teaches in place of tutorial prompts. When a player has ignored something important,
  the game lets it matter. The first occurrence shows the consequences without making the player
  feel them; the second gets no handicap.
